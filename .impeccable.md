## Design Context

### Users
Beginner and returning piano players who can follow sheet music but struggle to translate notation into a physical hand position. They use the interface at a piano, usually on a laptop or tablet, to understand where each finger belongs and to confirm the position by playing.

### Brand Personality
Patient, tactile, and precise. The interface should feel like a thoughtful teacher's marked-up music book: clear enough to trust, warm enough to practice with, and never childish.

### Aesthetic Direction
A light, paper-like workspace with ink, warm clay, and muted blue accents. Hand diagrams should feel illustrated rather than clinical, while the controls remain compact and utilitarian. Avoid game-like neon, generic dashboard cards, and ornamental music clichés.

### Design Principles
- Make thumb and pinky anchors obvious before showing finer detail.
- Keep the keyboard and hand as the visual center of gravity.
- Use color to explain state: assigned, pressed, and unassigned.
- Let users adjust the visual directly and see every change immediately.
- Preserve clarity at tablet widths for use on a music stand.
